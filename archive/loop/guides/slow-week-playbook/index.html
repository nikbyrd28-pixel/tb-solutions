What to do about a slow week

5 min read · For shop owners and chair renters

A slow week is not usually a marketing problem. It is a timing problem, and the difference matters — because marketing takes weeks to work and you need chairs filled on Thursday.

Everything below can be done between cuts, with a phone, for nothing.

1. Text the people who are already overdue

Before anything clever, go and get the clients you already have. Scroll your messages for regulars you have not seen in a while, and send one each — first name, no discount, one easy question.

Marcus — it's Trey at Maple Street. Been a minute, you're about due. Want me to get you in this week?

Ten of those will usually put two or three people in the chair, and they are people who already like your work. This is the single highest-return thing on the list, which is why it is first. There is a longer version of this in the win-back guide.

2. Book the next appointment before they stand up

This is the habit that prevents next month's slow week. While the cape is still on and he is looking at a fresh cut: "same time in three weeks?"

He is at his happiest with your work at that exact second, and it costs him nothing to say yes. Shops that do this have a calendar that fills itself; shops that do not are always starting from zero on Monday morning.

3. Fill the specific hole, not "the week"

"We're quiet" is too vague to act on. Look at the actual gaps. It is almost always the same shape: Tuesday and Wednesday mornings, and the last two hours before close.

Now you can offer something precise, which is far more persuasive than a general plea:

Got a cancellation at 2 today if you want it — first one to reply gets it.

Post that as a story, or send it to two or three people you know work nearby. Urgency that is true works; invented urgency gets ignored the third time you use it.

4. Post the work you did today, today

Not a graphic. Not a quote over a stock photo. The cut you finished twenty minutes ago, with the client's permission, straight from your phone.

Two practical notes that make a large difference:

  • Say where you are. Half the barbers posting good work never mention the town they are in. Put the town in the caption and tag the location.
  • Say how to book. Every post should end with the same short line and a link. People will not go hunting.

5. Go and be seen locally, in person

Slow hours are the only time you have to do this, which is exactly why it never gets done.

  • Walk your business cards or a small stack of flyers into the places your clients already are — the gym, the corner shop, the pizza place, the car wash.
  • Talk to the barber school if there is one nearby. Students need chairs and they bring their own clients.
  • Ask the shop next door if you can leave cards on their counter, and offer to do the same for them. Costs nothing and it is reciprocal, so people say yes.

What not to do in a slow week

  • Do not slash your prices. A discount to fill Tuesday teaches your regulars to wait for Tuesday. If you must move on price, tie it to something specific and temporary — a time slot, a first visit — never the standard cut.
  • Do not start a rebrand. New logo, new name, new sign: all of it feels productive and none of it puts anyone in the chair this week.
  • Do not sign up for ads on a panic Tuesday. Paid ads can work, but they need a budget you can sustain and a page to send people to. Fix the free things first — they are the same things that make ads work later.

The honest bit

Some weeks are slow for reasons that have nothing to do with you. The week after Christmas, the week schools go back, a week of rain, the week before payday. Barbers have always known this, and the shops that survive it are the ones who use the quiet hours to do the four things above instead of sitting on the chair looking at their phone.

The goal is not to never have a slow week. It is to make the next one shorter.
