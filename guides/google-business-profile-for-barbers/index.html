Getting found when somebody searches “barber near me”

7 min read · For shop owners

When somebody new in town wants a haircut, they do not go looking for barbershop websites. They open Google or Maps, type barber near me, and pick from the little block of three shops with a map above them. That block is called the map pack, and for a local barbershop it is more or less the entire internet.

Which means the thing that decides whether a stranger finds you is not your website, your Instagram, or how good your fades are. It is a free Google page called a Business Profile — and most shops have one they have never really filled in.

You are not competing to rank a website. You are competing to be one of three shops in a box.

First: claim the thing

Nearly every shop already has a profile whether they made one or not, because Google generates them from public data. Search your shop name. If you see it but there is a line saying "Own this business?", it is unclaimed — anybody could suggest edits to it and you would never know.

Claim it. Google will verify you are real, usually with a postcard, a phone call or a short video of your shop and street. It is tedious and it is worth doing properly once.

The fields that actually move you up the list

Google is trying to answer one question: if I send this person here, will they be happy? Everything below is you answering that question clearly.

Categories

Set the primary category to Barber shop. Not "hair salon", not "beauty salon" — those put you in a pool with places that do colour and nails, and you will lose searches you should win. Add secondary categories only if you genuinely do them.

Hours, including the awkward ones

Wrong hours are worse than no hours. Someone who drives over on a Monday you are closed does not shrug — they leave a one-star review about it. Set your real hours, and set special hours for holidays before they arrive.

Photos, and specifically the right kind

This is where shops leave the most on the table. Google shows profiles with real, recent photos more often, and people choose with their eyes. You want:

  • The storefront from the pavement, so they recognise it when they arrive. This one matters more than people think — it removes the small anxiety of walking into the wrong door.
  • The inside, so they know what they're walking into.
  • Actual work — fades, line-ups, beards. Post a few every week rather than thirty in one go.

Photos with faces need the client's OK. Most are flattered; ask anyway.

Services and prices

Fill in every service with a real price. People filter on price constantly, and a blank price field reads as "expensive" or "disorganised". It also means your prices show in the profile instead of someone having to call to ask.

The website field almost every shop leaves blank

There is a Website box on the profile. On most barbershop listings it is empty, because the owner does not have a site and assumes the field is optional. It is optional — and leaving it blank costs you twice.

It costs you the person who wanted to check your prices before committing, and it costs you a little of Google's confidence that you are an established business. It does not need to be a big website. It needs to be one page with your name, prices, hours, photos and a way to book.

Reviews, without begging

Reviews are the strongest thing you control, and asking badly is why most shops have nine of them.

Ask at the right moment. That moment is the twenty seconds after you spin the chair round and he likes what he sees. Not at the till, not by text three days later.

Make it one tap. Google gives every profile a short review link. Get it, turn it into a QR code, stick it on the mirror or the counter. "If you've got ten seconds, that code puts you straight on the review page" beats "you should leave us a review" every time.

Reply to all of them. Two lines is plenty. Replying tells Google the profile is live and tells the next reader that a human runs the place.

Answer the bad one calmly. A shop with all five stars looks fake. A shop with one three-star review and a polite, non-defensive reply underneath looks real. Never argue in public — you are not writing to the reviewer, you are writing to everyone who reads it later.

What not to do: never offer a free cut or a discount for a review. It is against Google's rules, reviews can be removed, and profiles can be penalised. Ask for honesty at a good moment — that is enough.

Posts, and why almost nobody bothers

Profiles have a Posts section that works a bit like a mini social feed. Hardly any barbershops use it, which is exactly why it is worth five minutes a week: a photo of a cut, one line, done. It keeps the profile looking active, and active profiles get shown.

Keep your details identical everywhere

Your shop name, address and phone number should be written exactly the same on Google, on your site, on Instagram, on Facebook. "12 W Gay St" in one place and "12 West Gay Street" in another is a small inconsistency, but multiply it across five sites and Google gets less sure you are one real business at one real address.

A realistic order to do this in

  1. Claim and verify the profile.
  2. Fix the category, hours and phone number.
  3. Add the storefront photo, then the inside, then five bits of work.
  4. Fill in services and prices.
  5. Put something in the website field.
  6. Get the review link, make it a QR, put it on the mirror.
  7. Post once a week and reply to every review.

None of this is clever and none of it costs money. It is just the part of the job that never feels urgent, because no client will ever walk in and tell you your Google listing is half empty — the ones affected by it are the ones who never showed up.
